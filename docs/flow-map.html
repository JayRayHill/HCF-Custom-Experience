What this is

The skeleton, before the skin

This is the cheap stage. Everything here is a decision that costs nothing to change now and a lot to change after it's built. The goal is to settle the shape of the experience — what screens exist, in what order, and what happens when someone goes off the happy path.

Two things are deliberately not settled yet. Branding — the Hot Cup Factory design system has landed and is held for stage 3 on purpose; this document is styled as a working document, not as a Hot Cup Factory artifact, so the structure gets judged on its own merits before the brand makes it look finished. And the catalog — product names and counts below are placeholders standing in for whatever your real custom range turns out to be. The structure is built to flex around it.

The entry

Two doors, one destination

The collection pages keep everything that earns them organic traffic — the banner copy, the SEO block, the FAQ. The only thing that changes is the module sitting where the HubSpot form is today. In its place: a fork.

The important property is that both doors end up in the same place. Someone who isn't ready to spec an order still becomes a lead, just a different kind, with the note attached that explains what they actually need.

The builder

Five screens, and two ways out

The forward path is short on purpose. Every screen either shows the customer something useful or asks for something we genuinely need — nothing exists to collect data the sales team won't use.

The two branches matter as much as the chain. Add more products is the attach-rate mechanism: someone who came for hot cups discovering that sleeves and lids are one tab over. Talk to us instead is the safety net: anyone who stalls still converts, and we find out where they stalled.

Screen by screen

What happens on each one

Read Exits as the important column. Most flow designs fail at the edges — the person who can't find their product, who wants less than the minimum, who closes the tab and comes back tomorrow.

ENTRY

The two doors

lives on the collection page
Sees
A module where the HubSpot form sits today. A short headline, then two clearly distinct paths — Build your custom order as the primary, Not sure what you need? Talk to us alongside it. The primary carries a plain promise: takes about two minutes, see minimums and lead times as you go.
We capture
Nothing yet. A click, tracked, so we can see the split between the two doors and tune the copy against real behaviour.
Exits
Into the builder, into the talk track, or further down the page to the existing SEO content and FAQ — all of which stays exactly where it is.
B1

Pick products

the screen that has to be good
Sees
Category tabs across the top, pre-selected based on where they came from but never locked. Below, a grid of product cards: real photography, name, the size range, the minimum order quantity, and the lead time. Opening a card reveals size rows with quantity steppers. A running summary stays visible the whole time — line items and total units.
We capture
Product, variant, and quantity for every line. Plus which tabs they opened, which is a quiet signal about interest they didn't act on.
Exits
Forward to review. Sideways to talk to us if the catalog doesn't have what they need. Or away entirely — in which case the list is waiting when they come back.
B2

Review list

Sees
Their selections as line items, grouped by category, with quantities editable in place. Anything below the minimum is flagged here rather than blocked earlier — with the minimum stated plainly and a route to talk it through.
We capture
The final line items, and any edits made at this stage — a quantity revised downward at review is worth knowing about.
Exits
Forward to project details, or back to add more products, which is where a single-category order becomes a multi-category one.
B3

Project details

the quote-enabling five
Sees
Five questions on one screen, no pagination: when do you need them in hand, where's your artwork (print-ready file / a logo somewhere / nothing yet), how many colors in the print, what's it for (café, event, franchise, reseller), and one-time or recurring.
We capture
All five. Only the date and artwork status are required — those two decide whether the order is even feasible. The rest improve the quote without gating it.
Exits
Forward to contact. Back to review with everything intact.
B4

Contact

Sees
Four fields: name, business name, email, phone. This is the only part of the experience that resembles the form we're replacing — four fields, at the end, after the customer has already invested effort worth protecting.
We capture
The contact record, joined to everything gathered on B1 through B3.
Exits
Submit. Or abandon here — which, with an email already typed, is the one abandonment worth recovering.
B5

Confirmation

Sees
What happens next, with a real timeframe rather than a vague thanks. Their full spec laid out so they can check it. A copy emailed to them. And a calendar link for anyone who'd rather talk now than wait for a rep to reach out.
We capture
Whether they booked time immediately — a strong buying signal worth routing on.
Exits
Back to the store, or into a booked call.
ALT

Talk to us

door two, and the escape hatch
Sees
A short form — name, email, phone, and an open box — beside the phone number and a booking calendar. Roughly what exists today, but aimed only at people it genuinely suits instead of everybody.
We capture
The contact and their note. Crucially, where they came from: the collection page, or a specific point in the builder. A rep picking up an escape-hatch lead knows what the customer was looking at when they gave up.
Exits
Into the same HubSpot record as everything else, tagged by route.
Open decisions

Six things I need you to call

These fork the design. Each one has a recommendation attached — tell me where you disagree and I'll rework the map before we touch wireframes.

DECISION 01

Do we show pricing?

Price ranges on the product cards build enormous trust and filter out unqualified leads before they reach a rep. They also commit you publicly to numbers that move with material costs, print complexity, and volume — and they hand competitors a price list.

Recommendation Show a per-unit starting point at the highest volume tier — "from $0.14/unit at 10,000" — rather than a full matrix. It anchors expectations and disqualifies the customer looking for 200 cups at wholesale, without publishing anything precise enough to shop against.
DECISION 02

What happens below the minimum order quantity?

Someone wants 250 custom hot cups and the minimum is 1,000. Three options: block them, let it through with a flag, or redirect them to stock product.

Recommendation Let it through, flagged at review with the minimum stated plainly, and offer the stock alternative right there. Blocking loses a lead who might have gone to 1,000 if a person had asked — and a rep can convert that conversation far better than a validation message can.
DECISION 03

Email early or email late?

Asking for an email up front means you can chase everyone who abandons. It also means a wall before the good part, and every abandoned-cart email you send is to someone who never saw what you offer.

Recommendation Late, at B4, with the list saved locally so returning customers pick up where they left off. You'll capture fewer emails and far more finished specs. If abandonment turns out to be the real problem, adding a save-my-progress prompt later is easy — removing a wall you've built the flow around is not.
DECISION 04

Do we accept artwork uploads in the flow?

It's tempting — the rep needs the file eventually. But an upload step mid-flow is a completion killer: files live on a designer's machine, not the phone someone is browsing on.

Recommendation Ask artwork status only, and take files later over email. Status is what determines the quote and the timeline; the file itself gets reviewed by a human before anything happens with it anyway.
DECISION 05

Does the entry point lock the category?

Someone arriving from the custom sleeve collection could see only sleeves, or see sleeves first with everything else available.

Recommendation Pre-select, never lock. This is the single biggest revenue argument for the whole project — today's siloed forms make it structurally impossible for a sleeve customer to tell you they also need cups.
DECISION 06

How many categories are we actually navigating?

Not a preference — a fact I need. Horizontal tabs work beautifully up to about six categories and fall apart past eight, at which point B1 needs a different navigation pattern entirely.

Recommendation Send me the list of custom product categories and roughly how many SKUs sit under each. If it's over eight, I'll redesign B1 around a sidebar or a filtered grid before we go near wireframes.

Design system received. Working from Fraunces and DM Sans, ink #161310, and the four accent families — Brew yellow, Iced teal for cold products, Roasted orange, and signal red — along with the usage rules: never two brand colors in one section, never a primary background at full width, ink-solid button as primary. If I've misread any of that, stage 3 is where it would show up, so worth correcting now.

Once the decisions are settled, stage 2 is wireframes — grey-box layouts of all seven screens, structure only. That's where we find out whether B1 can really hold a category grid, a quantity picker, and a running summary at once without feeling crowded on a phone.

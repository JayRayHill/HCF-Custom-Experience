The thesis

Two minutes.
Zero typing until the very end.

Every choice in the builder is a tap on something visual — a photograph, a cup silhouette, a volume chip. The keyboard doesn't appear until screen four, where we ask for a name and an email, and by then the customer has built something they don't want to abandon.

This is the whole design in one sentence, and it's a useful test to hold everything against. Any time a screen wants a text field, that's a signal we're asking the customer to do work the interface should be doing for them. A buyer who types "about 5,000 twelve ounce cups, maybe some lids too" into a box has given us a paragraph to interpret. A buyer who taps three things has given us a spec.

Why this matters more than it sounds. Your customers are café owners and operations managers browsing on a phone between other jobs. They are not going to compose prose about their packaging needs. Every text field you remove is a conversion point — and every tap you replace it with produces cleaner data than the sentence would have.

The constraint

A tap budget, not a vibe

"Quick" is worth nothing as an aspiration and everything as a number. Here's the target for the most common order in your catalog — one product, one size, one quantity, from landing on the collection page to a submitted spec.

Twelve taps is the budget — dropping the artwork question bought one back. Any feature that pushes it past about fifteen needs to earn its place by producing information the sales team will genuinely act on, and most won't. This is the number I'd hold the design to as it grows.

The rule

Nothing in here says buy

The builder has a shape borrowed from e-commerce — a grid, a running list, a continue button — and that shape is worth keeping, because it's the clearest way to show progress accumulating. But the words have to work against it, hard, on every single screen.

If a customer thinks they're building a cart, one of two bad things happens. Either the commitment feels too heavy for a first enquiry and they leave, or they finish expecting a checkout, don't get one, and feel misled at exactly the moment you wanted them to feel looked after. Both are avoidable with vocabulary.

Your site already has the right language and uses it well — Get a custom quote in the header, Request a Quote as step one of the published process. The builder should sound like the thing your customers were already promised, so the whole interface adopts it.

The moves

Nine decisions that make it fast

Each of these is a specific choice, and most of them have a worse obvious alternative that a lot of B2B configurators pick.

01

Volume chips, not a number input

The single most important interaction in the builder. A free number field means typing on a phone, an invisible minimum, and a validation error after the fact. A stepper means tapping plus forty-nine times to reach 5,000.

Preset chips solve all three at once: one tap, no keyboard, and the minimum is simply the first chip rather than a rule you discover by breaking it. It also matches how buyers actually think — in tiers and cases, not exact unit counts. Custom stays available for the buyer who genuinely needs 7,200.

02

The chips speak in cases

With pricing off, the chips still have to teach something, and cases are the honest answer. Your product pages already talk this way — 1,000 cups per case — and cases are how the order actually arrives on a loading dock. So each chip shows both: 3,000 above, 3 cases below.

Every tier is deliberately a whole number of cases. A customer never picks a quantity that turns into two and a half boxes, which is the kind of small correctness that makes a tool feel like it was built by people who know the product.

03

Sizes shown at true relative scale

Cup sizes are physical objects, so draw them as physical objects. The silhouettes in the size picker are proportioned to the real cups — an 8 oz really is about 60% the height of a 20 oz.

A buyer who isn't fluent in ounces can pick by eye in a way a row of text labels never allows. This is the clearest example of "visual and informative" in the whole build, and it's specific to what you sell — it wouldn't work for a company selling software.

04

Nothing navigates away

Opening a product expands a panel in place. No modal, no new page, no back button. The grid stays where it was, the summary stays visible, and the scroll position never jumps.

The failure mode this avoids is the one that kills most catalog flows: tap a product, land on a detail page, hit back, lose your place, give up. Inline expansion means browsing four products costs four taps and no disorientation.

05

The minimum is a floor that can't be crossed

You ruled that sub-minimum quantities shouldn't be possible, and the chip design delivers that for free: the lowest chip is the minimum, and Custom is floored at the same number. An invalid order is unconstructible, so no validation message ever has to fire.

Worth naming the trade you've made. A café wanting 250 printed cups now has no path forward inside the builder — they'll land on talk to us instead. That's the right call if those enquiries mostly waste your reps' time, and the wrong one if some meaningful share of them grow into real orders once a person is on the phone. The escape hatch catches them either way; they just aren't invited in.

06

Progress is always visible

The running order sits in a rail on desktop and a sticky bar on mobile — never behind a cart icon with a badge. Seeing the list build is what motivates finishing it, and hiding it behind a tap costs you exactly the customers who were on the fence.

It's also the quiet mechanism for multi-category orders: a visible list with room in it invites another line far more effectively than a prompt would.

07

Only decision-critical specs on the card face

Minimum and lead time go on the card, because they're the two facts that eliminate products. Material, wall construction, certifications, and print method live behind a Full specs toggle in the expanded panel.

The customer choosing between three cup types needs two numbers, not two paragraphs. The detail is there for the buyer who wants it — it just doesn't tax the eleven who don't.

08

The tabs never lock

Arriving from the sleeve collection pre-selects the sleeve tab. It doesn't hide the others, and the tab row is the first thing on the screen — your call, and the right one.

This is the revenue argument for the entire project. Today a sleeve enquiry is structurally incapable of becoming a sleeve-and-cup enquiry, because the form only knows about sleeves. One tab row fixes that. With five categories the whole range is visible at once without a single scroll.

09

Every dead end has a door

Empty categories, no matches, below minimum, product we don't make — each of those states carries an action rather than a message. Usually talk to us, occasionally a stock alternative, never a shrug.

An escape hatch that captures where the customer was standing when they used it is worth more than a perfect flow, because it tells you what to build next.

Screen B3

Four questions, still no keyboard

This is where the flow is most at risk of turning back into the form we're replacing. Four open text fields would undo all of it. So every answer is a chip or a card — and because the options are fixed, the sales team gets clean, sortable values instead of free text they have to read.

Only the in-hands date is required. The other three improve the quote without gating it.

Mobile

Where this actually gets used

Assume most of your custom traffic is on a phone, and design B1 for that first. Three changes, none of them structural:

—

The rail becomes a sticky bottom bar

Collapsed it reads 2 products · 7,500 units with a continue button. Tapping it raises a sheet with the full list. Progress stays visible, which is the property that matters — it just uses the one piece of screen furniture phones handle well.

—

Two-up grid, and the tabs scroll

Product cards stay photo-led at two across — one across wastes the screen and makes the catalog feel smaller than it is. The tab row scrolls horizontally and sticks to the top as the grid moves under it.

—

The expanded panel is unchanged

Silhouettes and chips are already touch targets at a comfortable size, so the panel needs no mobile variant at all. That's the payoff for choosing chips over number inputs — the interaction was built for thumbs from the start.

Site continuity

Three things the live site already gives us

The screenshots changed my thinking in a few places. The builder shouldn't arrive as a novelty — it should slot into a story your site already tells well.

—

The builder is step one

You already publish a three-step promise: Request a Quote → Custom Design Proof → We Print & Ship. The builder isn't a new process, it's a better version of step one — so the confirmation screen should show that same three-step graphic with step one marked done.

That's a free and very effective "what happens next", because it's the sequence the customer already read on the way in.

—

The sample box belongs on the confirmation screen

"Specs on a screen only tell you so much" is your own copy, and it's exactly right about a builder made of silhouettes and chips. Someone who has just specced 5,000 cups is the single best candidate for a free sample box you already offer.

One button on B5. It costs nothing, it fills the wait before a rep calls, and it puts your product in the hands of a lead at their moment of highest intent.

—

"Get a custom quote" is already in your header

That button exists on every page and currently goes to a form. Pointing it at the builder gives the new experience site-wide reach without designing a single new entry point.

Worth deciding deliberately though — it's a bigger behavioural change than swapping the module on five collection pages, and it's the one that will move the numbers most.

Where this stands

All six decisions from stage 1 are now closed: no pricing, hard minimums, email late at B4, no artwork step, categories pre-select but never lock, and five categories — coffee cups, PET, cold paper cups, sleeves, mason jars. Plus one more, added since: quote vocabulary throughout, never cart vocabulary. All of it is reflected above.
